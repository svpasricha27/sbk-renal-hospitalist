01

Orientation, admitting a dialysis patient, and why they come in

About 20 minutes  ·  5 checkpoints  ·  certificate

Start with what the machine is doing

Almost everything that feels unfamiliar about this ward follows from one fact. The kidney's job is being done intermittently by a machine three times a week instead of continuously by an organ.

Blood leaves the patient through a needle or a catheter and is pumped at around 300 to 400 mL per minute into a dialyser. Inside the dialyser sit thousands of hollow fibres made of a semipermeable membrane, and the blood runs through the middle of those fibres. On the outside of them runs a fluid called dialysate, flowing in the opposite direction. Blood and dialysate never actually mix. They simply travel alongside each other, separated by a membrane, for the three or four hours the patient is on the machine.

What that gets you

Waste products move out of the blood and into the dialysate, and fluid comes off at the same time. How much fluid comes off is decided by nephrology for each session, worked out from the weight the patient arrives at compared with the weight they are meant to be. That second number is the target weight and you will hear it constantly.

Two consequences matter for you. Potassium, phosphate and acid balance are largely set by what nephrology puts in the dialysate, which is why supplementing electrolytes is rarely the right first move on this ward. And because all of this happens in bursts rather than continuously, nothing about a dialysis patient sits at a steady state.

Why Mondays are busy

Between runs, everything the kidney would normally excrete piles up. Potassium climbs, fluid accumulates, acid builds. The run brings it all back down over four hours, and then it starts climbing again. Nothing about a dialysis patient sits at a steady state, which is why a potassium drawn an hour after a run tells you very little about where it will be tomorrow morning.

Across the weekend that two day gap becomes a three day gap. An extra 24 hours of accumulation is exactly what you would expect to be dangerous, and it is. Hyperkalemia, pulmonary edema and sudden cardiac death all cluster at the end of the long interval. If you are admitting an MWF patient on a Monday or a TTS patient on a Tuesday, assume you are meeting them at the worst point of their week.

What dialysis does not do

A standard schedule replaces perhaps 10 to 15 percent of native kidney function, and only in bursts. It does nothing at all for the endocrine work the kidney used to do. That is why these patients need erythropoietin, why they need activated vitamin D (calcitriol) and phosphate binders, and why their blood pressure never quite settles. Modules 2 and 4 deal with each of those in turn.

Checkpoint 1 of 5
A patient on Monday Wednesday Friday hemodialysis is admitted on a Monday morning. Which feature of the schedule best explains why Monday admissions tend to be sicker?

The long gap. Two days without dialysis becomes three across the weekend. An extra 24 hours of potassium, fluid and acid accumulation is enough to tip patients over, and hyperkalemia, pulmonary edema and sudden cardiac death all cluster at the end of that interval.

The practical version of this is simple. When you meet a dialysis patient at the start of the week, you are meeting them at their worst, and the same patient two days later will look considerably better without you having done anything.

Protecting the veins

Every peripheral vein in the arm of a dialysis patient is a possible future access. Cannulating them repeatedly scars them, and a line sitting in one occludes it. Damage done during a medical admission can quietly close off the option of a fistula years later and push someone toward a permanent catheter, which carries much worse long term outcomes. None of this means you cannot get access when you need it. It means the threshold is different from the one you are used to.

  • PICC lines only when genuinely necessary. They sit in exactly the veins a future fistula needs. If prolonged intravenous access looks likely, speak to nephrology first, because quite often the answer turns out to be a different plan rather than a different line.
  • Avoid the subclavian route where you have an alternative. Subclavian cannulation is the classic cause of central venous stenosis, which shows up years later as a swollen arm that no diuretic will fix. Internal jugular is preferred if a central line is needed.
  • Reach for dorsal hand veins first for peripheral IVs and for bloods, and use the forearm and antecubital fossa only when there is no reasonable alternative.
  • Nothing goes in the access arm. No IV, no blood pressure cuff, no venipuncture, no tourniquet.

Bloodwork

Daily bloods are a habit worth breaking here. Every draw costs a vein you are trying to protect, and the cumulative blood loss is a real contributor to anemia in a population that already needs erythropoietin to make red cells. For a stable inpatient, timing bloodwork to the dialysis days is almost always enough, and it lines up with the routine draws the unit is already doing. Take samples before the run rather than after, because potassium, urea, phosphate and calcium all rebound over the following hours and a post run sample will mislead you.

You cannot interpret a result without knowing when it was drawn relative to dialysis. That one piece of context changes what the number means and what you should do about it, so get into the habit of asking before you act on anything.

Prophylaxis against clots

Sunnybrook renal ward practice

It depends on the modality. Hemodialysis patients get enoxaparin 30 mg SC daily, which is the reduced renal dose rather than the usual 40 mg. Peritoneal dialysis patients get unfractionated heparin 5000 units SC BID.

If the patient is already on therapeutic anticoagulation, meaning warfarin, a DOAC or treatment dose low molecular weight heparin, they do not need separate prophylaxis on top. Ordering both is common and the pharmacist ends up clarifying it, so it saves everyone time to get it right the first time.

Do not leave prophylaxis off on the assumption that the dialysis circuit anticoagulation carries over, because it does not last between sessions. Weigh it against bleeding risk the way you would for anyone else, keeping in mind that uremic platelet dysfunction raises that risk.

Checkpoint 2 of 5
You are writing admission orders for a 70 year old on maintenance hemodialysis. Which set is right?

Thirty milligrams, and labs timed to the run. Enoxaparin 30 mg daily is the reduced renal dose used here for hemodialysis patients. The dialysis circuit anticoagulation does not persist between runs, so it does not substitute for prophylaxis.

Daily bloodwork wastes veins and adds up to real blood loss in someone who needs erythropoietin to replace it. Drawing after the run is worse still, because potassium, urea, phosphate and calcium all rebound over the next few hours and the numbers you get will look better than the patient is.

Checkpoint 3 of 5
The lab calls at 14:00 with a potassium of 3.1 on one of your hemodialysis patients. What do you do first?

Ask when it was taken. You cannot interpret any result on this ward without that, and potassium is the clearest example.

Drawn before the run, it is a real value. If it is 3.0 or below, give a low dose of oral potassium, roughly half what you would normally prescribe. Above 3.0, leave it alone.

Drawn during or just after the run, ignore it. Potassium rebounds out of the cells over the following hours and the number will look completely different by the evening. Repeat it later in the day if you are concerned.

The same logic applies to urea, phosphate and calcium, which is why the sample should be taken before the run whenever you intend to act on it.

Pulling apart weakness and falls

This is the commonest soft admission on the ward and the one most likely to go home unchanged. It is worth working through deliberately, because almost every contributor is treatable.

  • The dialysis itself. Dropping blood pressure during the run leaves patients wiped out for hours afterwards, and many describe losing most of the day after each session. Ask the unit for the pressures during the run and the pre and post weights. Module 2 deals with this in detail.
  • Their medications. Gabapentin and pregabalin are the single commonest reversible cause of drowsiness, unsteadiness and falls on this ward. Both are cleared by the kidney, and the doses these patients need are markedly lower than the doses used for the same indications in anyone else. Ask the nephrology pharmacist for guidance on the dose. After that, look at opioids, since morphine and codeine accumulate, then baclofen, sedatives and the timing of antihypertensives. Module 4 has the full list.
  • Anemia. Check that the hemoglobin is at target and has actually been worked up rather than assumed. Module 4.
  • The heart. Ischemia, arrhythmia, aortic stenosis, and autonomic neuropathy in long standing diabetes.
  • Nutrition. Protein energy wasting is common here and consistently under recognized. A dietitian referral is not optional.
The fall you must not miss

A hemodialysis patient who falls has had repeated heparin exposure and has platelet dysfunction on top of it. Subdural hematoma in this group presents late and atypically, often as confusion, headache or simply being not quite themselves several days later. Keep the threshold for a CT head low, and image again if the story does not fit.

Checkpoint 5 of 5
An 80 year old on hemodialysis is admitted after two falls at home. She takes gabapentin 300 mg three times daily for itch, ramipril, and hydromorphone. Which step is likely to help most?

The gabapentin. Nine hundred milligrams a day of a renally cleared drug in someone with no kidney function is a large dose, and drowsiness, unsteadiness and falls are exactly what it produces. It gets prescribed at ordinary doses for itch and neuropathy all the time, and it is the first thing worth looking at whenever a dialysis patient is vague and off their feet.

Hydromorphone is the preferred opioid here, so changing her to morphine would make things worse rather than better. If she needs treatment for osteoporosis, that is a conversation with the nephrology pharmacist and the nephrologist rather than something to start on the ward. And with two falls plus regular heparin exposure, keep subdural hematoma on your list.

02

Volume management and blood pressure control

About 18 minutes  ·  4 checkpoints  ·  certificate

Part one, volume

Nearly everything on this ward routes back to volume. Get the volume assessment right and the blood pressure, the breathlessness and a good share of the falls sort themselves out.

What target weight actually is

Target weight is the weight at which a patient is judged to be euvolemic. In practice it is the lowest weight they can be brought down to without cramping or dropping their blood pressure. The ultrafiltration goal for any given run is just the weight they arrived at minus their target weight, plus whatever fluid goes in during the session.

The important thing to understand is that target weight is an estimate rather than a measurement. Nobody weighs the patient's extracellular fluid. Somebody watched how they tolerated sessions over several weeks and settled on a number. That number stays in the chart until someone changes it, and meanwhile the patient's actual tissue mass keeps moving.

Assessing volume on the ward

Weight against target weight is the most useful single number you have, provided you know what the target is. Add the jugular venous pressure, the chest, the peripheral edema, and point of care ultrasound for B lines if you use it. Then pick up the phone. The two questions that tell you most are whether the patient is reaching their target weight and whether they drop their pressure during runs.

Interdialytic weight gain matters as well. More than about 4 percent of body weight between runs means a large volume has to come off in a fixed four hours, which forces a high ultrafiltration rate and sets up the problems in part three. The main driver is sodium rather than fluid alone, because salt drives the thirst that drives the drinking.

Two questions that explain most unexpected gains

What are they taking. NSAIDs cause fluid retention and are worth asking about directly, since patients often do not count over the counter medication as medication.

Where is the fluid coming from. Ask whether family are bringing drinks in, and whether the patient is getting fluids elsewhere in the hospital while off the ward. This comes up constantly and it will not show up anywhere in your chart.

Checkpoint 1 of 4
A patient recovering from a two week admission with poor oral intake now cramps and drops her pressure at the end of every run. Her target weight of 74 kg was set before she came in. What is going on?

The target has drifted. Two weeks of barely eating means genuine loss of muscle and fat, so the weight that once represented euvolemia now represents hypovolemia. The machine is pulling her below her real dry weight, which is exactly why she cramps and drops her pressure at the end of each session.

Flag it to nephrology so the target can be moved up, usually in steps of 0.2 to 0.5 kg over several runs. Reaching for midodrine here would be treating a problem you are creating unnecessarily.

Part two, hypertension

In hemodialysis, high blood pressure is a volume problem until proven otherwise. Adding a fourth agent before dealing with salt and water is the classic mistake.

The order of operations is volume, then sodium, then drugs. Probing the target weight downward is the first move, and it is worth warning the team that the response lags. Blood pressure can take weeks to fall after a patient is genuinely euvolemic, so do not decide the approach has failed on day three. Sodium restriction matters more than fluid restriction, because cutting salt cuts the thirst that produces the weight gain in the first place.

Only once those two are addressed does drug choice become the interesting question, and here the choice really does depend on how each agent behaves during a run.

Please do not do this

Do not treat an asymptomatic high pre dialysis blood pressure with intravenous hydralazine or labetalol. The pre dialysis reading is the least informative measurement of the whole interdialytic period, it is usually volume driven, and the patient is about to have several litres taken off them. What follows predictably is a pressure crash during the run, a session cut short, inadequate dialysis and often a fall.

A patient sitting comfortably at 188 over 96 before their run needs their target weight reviewed. They do not need an intravenous push.

You want to add a beta blocker for a patient on Monday Wednesday Friday hemodialysis. Which is the usual first choice here?

Bisoprolol at bedtime. It is covered, it is not heavily dialyzed, and it is once daily. Giving it at night matters more than people expect, because its onset is well past by the time a daytime run starts, which makes the question of how dialyzable it is largely moot.

Part three, when the pressure falls during the run

This is the other half of the blood pressure problem and it causes a great deal of what you will see. It cuts sessions short, leaves patients underdialyzed, and repeatedly stuns the myocardium.

The mechanism is worth holding onto because it explains every treatment. Ultrafiltration pulls water out of the vascular space. Fluid then refills that space from the interstitium, but only at a certain rate. As long as removal is slower than refilling, the patient stays stable. Push the removal rate above the refill rate and the intravascular volume falls, and the pressure follows. Everything that helps either slows the removal, moves the endpoint up, or supports the vasculature while it happens.

What causes it

  • An ultrafiltration rate that is too high, usually because a large interdialytic gain has to be squeezed into a fixed four hours
  • A target weight set too low, or a true dry weight that has risen as nutrition improved
  • Antihypertensives given on the morning of the run
  • Eating during dialysis, which diverts blood to the gut
  • Sepsis, bleeding, anemia, low albumin, adrenal insufficiency
  • Cardiac disease, particularly diastolic dysfunction, aortic stenosis, arrhythmia or a pericardial effusion
  • Autonomic neuropathy, especially in long standing diabetes

What you can do from the ward

Start with timing, because it is free and it is frequently the whole problem. Hold morning antihypertensives on dialysis days or move them to after the run, and write that explicitly into the orders, because it will not happen on its own.

If timing and target weight do not fix it, midodrine is the usual next step. It is an alpha 1 agonist given 15 to 30 minutes before the run, starting at 2.5 to 5 mg and titrating up to 10 mg per dose. Give it on dialysis days only, since it is not a maintenance antihypotensive. Warn patients about supine hypertension and avoid dosing it close to lying flat for the night. Scalp tingling and goosebumps are common and entirely harmless, but they alarm people who were not told.

Finally, look for the medical cause hiding underneath. Occult sepsis, a slow GI bleed and a new pericardial effusion all announce themselves first as a patient who keeps dropping their pressure on the machine.

Checkpoint 3 of 4
Your inpatient has needed her run stopped early three sessions in a row. She takes amlodipine 10 mg and carvedilol 12.5 mg twice daily, both given at 08:00 with her other morning medications, and her run starts at 09:00. What do you change first?

Timing first. Two antihypertensives given an hour before a session is a problem we created ourselves and the cheapest one to fix. Move them to after the run and see what happens before doing anything else. Then flag the pattern to nephrology, who will look at the target weight and the ultrafiltration rate.

Midodrine is reasonable if timing and target weight do not solve it, but it is given at 2.5 to 10 mg fifteen to thirty minutes before the run on dialysis days only, not three times daily. Pre emptive saline simply adds volume that has to come off again in the same session.

Putting the two halves together

The same patient can be overloaded and hypotensive on the machine at the same time, and that combination is common rather than paradoxical. A large gain between runs forces a high removal rate, the removal outpaces the refill, and the session gets cut short before the fluid is off. They then arrive at the next run even more overloaded than the last.

Breaking that cycle almost never involves a new drug in either direction. It involves a longer or extra session, a smaller sodium load so the gain is smaller to begin with, and an honest reappraisal of the target weight.

If the patient still makes urine, this is where a loop diuretic earns its place. Regular furosemide on the non dialysis days reduces how much accumulates between runs, so there is less to take off at the next session and the removal rate does not have to be as high. We use that tactic often, and it is easy to forget in someone who is on dialysis.

Three questions for the dialysis unit
  1. Is she reaching her target weight, or is the goal being cut short?
  2. What do her pressures do during the run, and does she cramp?
  3. What is her average interdialytic weight gain?

Between them these tell you whether you are looking at a volume problem, a target weight problem or a cardiovascular one, and none of the three is fixed the same way.

Checkpoint 4 of 4
Pre dialysis blood pressure is 192 over 98. The patient is comfortable, talking, not breathless, and due on the machine in 40 minutes. What do you do?

Leave it alone. A pre dialysis reading is the least informative number of the whole interdialytic period, it is usually volume driven, and this patient is about to have litres removed. Lowering it acutely converts an asymptomatic number into a real harm, because what follows is a pressure crash on the machine, a session stopped early and a patient who goes home still overloaded.

Cancelling the run is worse again, since it guarantees they arrive at the next one with even more fluid on board. Review the target weight, the sodium intake and the gain between runs instead.

Dialysis access, catheter infections, and antimicrobial dosing

About 20 minutes  ·  4 checkpoints  ·  certificate

Part one, knowing the access

You are not expected to manage dialysis access. You are expected to know which sort your patient has, to protect it, and to recognize the handful of situations that need someone paged straight away.

To get 300 to 400 mL of blood per minute out of a patient and back again, you need something considerably bigger than an ordinary vein. There are three ways of arranging that, and they differ enormously in how long they last and how often they get infected.

What to check, and what to leave alone

AccessYour daily checkWhat you are not doing
Fistula or graft Feel for a thrill and listen for a bruit once a day. A working access buzzes continuously under your fingers, and it takes about fifteen seconds to confirm. Part two, three things that need you

Most access problems are somebody else's to manage. These three are worth knowing cold, because a hospitalist is usually the first person to see them.

Emergency drill, click through itBleeding from a fistula or graft

An aneurysmal segment of a fistula or graft can rupture, and the flow through it is arterial. A patient can bleed to death from this in minutes. It is worth clicking through the sequence now so that you already know it if it ever happens.

Step 1, immediately
Press. Do not go looking for gauze first.

Put two fingers straight onto the bleeding point with firm pressure, through whatever happens to be to hand. Press on the hole itself rather than around it, so that you occlude the puncture while leaving flow going through the access. Do not wrap anything all the way round the limb.

Step 2, at the same moment
Call for help. Pull the emergency bell.

This is a code level event rather than a page the resident event. Pull the emergency call bell, get a second person into the room, and have them page nephrology and vascular surgery while you keep pressing. Do not leave the bedside and do not let go to have a look.

Step 3, within a minute
Elevate the arm. Keep pressing.

Raise the limb above the level of the heart and maintain uninterrupted pressure. The commonest mistake is lifting off after thirty seconds to check whether it has stopped. Get large bore intravenous access in the other arm, send a crossmatch, and treat this as a major hemorrhage.

Step 4, only if pressure fails
Tourniquet above the site.

This is a last resort and only if direct pressure is not controlling it. Put a tourniquet proximal to the bleeding point and note the time. Doing so will probably cost the access, which is the right trade when the alternative is exsanguination. Vascular surgery takes over from there.

Step 5, the version you can prevent
Recognize the rupture that has not happened yet.

These almost always announce themselves first. Shiny, thinned or ulcerated skin over an aneurysmal segment, or a scab that keeps re forming in the same spot, means the tissue holding back arterial pressure is failing. That needs urgent vascular review the same day, before it bleeds. Spotting this on a round is one of the few access findings where a hospitalist genuinely changes the outcome.

Steal syndrome, in more detail

A fistula or graft is a low resistance channel between an artery and a vein. Blood takes the path of least resistance, so a large share of the arterial flow that used to travel down the arm and into the hand now runs straight into the access and back to the heart instead. Most people compensate for that by dilating the smaller collateral arteries around the elbow and forearm, and they never notice anything. Patients who cannot compensate, typically those with diabetes or peripheral vascular disease whose distal arteries are calcified and cannot dilate, end up with a genuinely ischemic hand. In the more severe cases the flow in the artery beyond the anastomosis actually reverses and runs backwards into the access.

It shows itself during dialysis first, because that is when flow through the access is at its highest. The usual sequence runs from pain, coldness and pins and needles that only happen on the machine, through rest pain and persistent numbness, to weakness and visible wasting of the small muscles of the hand, and finally to ulceration and tissue loss at the fingertips.

There is a useful bedside test. Compress the access firmly with your fingers for a few seconds and watch what happens. If the radial pulse strengthens and the pain settles, you have diverted flow back into the hand and effectively confirmed the diagnosis. Risk is higher with an upper arm access taken off the brachial artery, with diabetes and peripheral vascular disease, with increasing age, and in patients who have already had several accesses. It can appear within hours of the access being created or years later.

Refer urgently rather than watching it, and treat any motor weakness or tissue loss as a threatened limb needing review the same day. Referral does not mean the patient loses their access, which is worth telling them, because several operations restore flow to the hand while keeping the access working.

Central venous stenosis, in more detail

Anything sitting in a central vein injures the lining of that vein. The repair process lays down scar tissue and thickens the wall, and over months the vein narrows. The subclavian route is much worse than the internal jugular, because the vein is squeezed between the clavicle and the first rib and moves with every shoulder movement. PICC lines do the same thing further out in the axillary and subclavian veins. All of this is usually completely silent while the arm is only draining at normal flow, so the patient has no symptoms and nobody knows it has happened.

The problem surfaces when a fistula or graft is created downstream of the narrowing. Several hundred millilitres a minute now have to get out through a vein that will not take it. The arm swells, and if the obstruction is high enough the breast, neck and face on that side swell too. Dilated veins appear across the chest wall and shoulder as blood finds its way back through collaterals.

Alongside the swelling you may hear about bleeding that goes on for a long time after the needles come out, high venous pressures and repeated alarms on the machine, and dialysis that is simply not clearing well. The reflex to treat a swollen arm as a deep vein thrombosis is understandable, and thrombosis can certainly sit on top of a stenosis, but a diuretic will do nothing at all here. The investigation is venography, usually with angioplasty done at the same sitting and sometimes a stent, and it does tend to recur, so patients come back for repeat procedures.

When it affects both sides, or the superior vena cava itself, it can close off access options across the whole upper body and leave a patient dependent on a catheter for good. That is the reason this comes up in Module 1 as a reason to think carefully before requesting a PICC.

Part four, dosing antimicrobials

Two habits cover most of this. Give the dose after the run wherever a post dialysis regimen exists, and look the agent up rather than assuming, because a surprising number of common antibiotics need no adjustment at all and a couple should not be used.

Before any of that, call the nephrology pharmacist

Contact them at the start of any antimicrobial course, and any time you are in doubt. There is far more nuance in this than a lookup table can hold, and getting the first dose right is much easier than fixing it later.

Anything that needs levels, meaning vancomycin, the aminoglycosides and voriconazole, should be discussed before the first dose so that dosing and monitoring are set up properly from the outset.

Why after the run matters so much

Giving a drug once dialysis has finished stops the dialyser clearing it straight back out again. Just as importantly, it means the dose can be given by the dialysis nurse in the unit while the patient is already there. No extra intravenous access, no additional visits after discharge, and adherence that is close to perfect. A patient who needs a fortnight of cefazolin for staph aureus can finish the entire course without a single extra appointment, which is a considerable difference to their life.

Write it into the order

Order it as give after HD in unit, MWF or on dialysis days give towards the end of HD. A plain daily order will be given on the ward at the wrong time and then dialyzed straight off. Involve renal pharmacy on admission and they will line the schedule up with the runs.

Part one, anemia

Healthy kidneys make erythropoietin, and failing ones do not. That is the mechanism. In practice though, the reason a hemoglobin falls on your ward is usually iron, blood loss or inflammation rather than the erythropoietin itself.

So a new or worsening anemia in a dialysis patient is not automatically their kidney disease. Check a full blood count with reticulocytes, ferritin, transferrin saturation, B12 and folate, and think hard about occult bleeding from the gut, since angiodysplasia and uremic platelet dysfunction together make that common. Hemolysis, myeloma and malignancy all still exist. So does blood lost into the circuit and blood lost to your own phlebotomy.

Read the trend, not the number

A stable hemoglobin is handled completely differently from one that has moved, so always look back at the previous values before deciding anything.

Repeat any reading that is out of keeping with the ones before it. One off aberrant values genuinely happen. Volume also moves the number on its own, so an overloaded patient can look falsely anemic and a dry one falsely better than they are.

The rate of change matters as much as the value. A rise from 80 to 120 over three months is a different problem from the same rise over a week, and the second one needs the ESA held rather than trimmed. If you see a jump of 20 or more, flag it to the nephrology pharmacist.

Transfusion

Time it with dialysis when you can, but never let that delay an emergency

A unit of red cells is around 300 mL of volume plus a potassium load, going into someone who may not pass urine at all. Where a transfusion is not urgent, we time it to run during a dialysis session or immediately before one, so the volume comes off in the same sitting and the potassium is cleared with it.

If the patient is bleeding or unstable, transfuse now. Do not hold blood back waiting for a dialysis slot. Call nephrology, tell them what you have given, and they will arrange an extra session the same day to deal with the volume and the potassium. That is a routine request rather than an imposition.

Part two, bone and mineral disorder

You will not be managing this. The drugs are genuinely confusing, they interact with one another, and the targets are contested even among nephrologists. What you need is to recognize the medications, know roughly what each is for, and get them onto the admission reconciliation correctly. Nephrology and renal pharmacy do the rest.

The disease in one paragraph

Failing kidneys cannot excrete phosphate and cannot activate vitamin D. Phosphate climbs, calcium falls, and the parathyroid glands respond by working harder, so parathyroid hormone rises. Over years that produces abnormal bone, either high turnover disease or, if the hormone is suppressed too far, a low turnover state called adynamic bone. Alongside the bone changes, calcium and phosphate deposit progressively in arteries and heart valves. The vascular calcification is what actually kills these patients, which is why treatment aims at the phosphate and calcium load rather than at bone density.

The targets, and how uncertain they are

Phosphate. There is no proven target, and the trial evidence behind any particular number is weak. Guidelines say only to bring it toward the normal range. Most of us aim for a phosphate below about 1.8 mmol/L, and below 2.0 is a reasonable working threshold on the inpatient side where it is not something to fuss over. Either way it is the trend that matters more than any single value.

Calcium. Keep it inside the normal range, corrected for albumin, and avoid hypercalcemia.

Parathyroid hormone. Roughly two to nine times the upper limit of normal for whichever assay is being used. Both extremes cause harm, and a completely normal parathyroid hormone in a dialysis patient is not a good result, because suppressing it that far produces adynamic bone, fractures and worse vascular calcification.

Two things not to start on this ward
  • Denosumab. Do not start it. It causes severe, prolonged and occasionally fatal hypocalcemia in dialysis patients, which is now a boxed warning. It is not on our hospital formulary, so it cannot simply be continued, and stopping it abruptly without another plan raises fracture risk sharply. Some patients are on it as established therapy and the pharmacist will sort out what happens next.
  • Bisphosphonates. Not something you would start on the ward, but do not assume they are off limits. Part three, electrolytes

One question sits underneath almost every electrolyte decision here. Will the next run fix this, or make it worse?

Potassium

Start by taking an ECG and giving intravenous calcium if there are changes. After that, the order of operations here is not the one you are used to.

  1. Dialysis, first. In a patient who passes no urine this is the only definitive treatment, and getting them onto the machine is the intervention. Our patients also tolerate higher potassium levels than the general population, and plenty of them sit around 6 as outpatients without anyone treating it as an emergency.
  2. Then furosemide, if they still make urine, or a potassium binder such as sodium zirconium cyclosilicate.

Shifting the potassium and then dialysing shortly afterwards is a common sequence from the emergency department and it overcorrects, because the run removes potassium that has already been driven into the cells. If dialysis is coming, let dialysis do the work.

Expect a rebound after the run as potassium moves back out of cells, which is why a level drawn immediately afterwards is falsely reassuring. And always ask why it happened, because a missed run, constipation, a GI bleed or a stoppable drug will usually explain it.

A low potassium in a hemodialysis patient is uncommon and always deserves an explanation. Diuretics are a common culprit, alongside poor intake, diarrhea, refeeding and a dialysate potassium set too low. Replete cautiously and recheck, since the definitive fix is normally a dialysate change and that is nephrology's call. Peritoneal dialysis is the opposite, where a low potassium is common and often needs regular oral supplementation.

If insulin and dextrose have already been given

The kidney clears a meaningful share of circulating insulin, so in kidney failure the insulin outlasts the dextrose you gave with it. Hypoglycemia therefore turns up one to six hours later, long after the acute event is over and everyone has moved on to something else. Follow the insulin with a dextrose infusion and monitor glucose hourly for at least six hours.

The same physiology explains why insulin requirements generally fall in kidney failure, and why HbA1c is unreliable in these patients, since red cell survival is shortened and erythropoietin distorts it further. Use glucose logs rather than the A1c.

Sodium, magnesium, phosphate and acid base

  • Sodium. Hyponatremia here is nearly always dilutional, so the treatment is fluid restriction and ultrafiltration rather than saline. Do not correct it yourself. Dialysis drops the urea and raises the sodium quickly, and the two osmolar shifts together risk osmotic demyelination. Nephrology tailors the dialysate sodium for exactly this reason.
  • Magnesium is usually low here, not high. Our dialysate does not have magnesium as an option, unlike some other centres, and magnesium is dialyzed, so a great many of our patients run low. Replace it intravenously, not orally. Oral magnesium gives most of them significant diarrhea, which then worsens the hypomagnesemia, and you have created a loop. Nephrology can arrange intravenous replacement during a run.
  • Phosphate is usually high, but a low phosphate in hospital is real and comes from refeeding, malnutrition or intensified daily dialysis. Check first that the sample was drawn before the run. If it is genuinely low, the usual first step is to reduce or hold the phosphate binders, meaning calcium, sevelamer, lanthanum or sucroferric oxyhydroxide, and to feed the patient. Discuss it with the pharmacist rather than working out the binder changes yourself.
  • Acidosis is expected and is managed by the bicarbonate in the dialysate. Oral sodium bicarbonate carries a sodium and volume load, so coordinate with nephrology rather than starting it alone.

Watch the glucose for six hours. The kidney normally clears a good share of circulating insulin. Without that, the insulin outlasts the dextrose and hypoglycemia typically appears one to six hours later, once the acute event has finished and attention has moved elsewhere. Living with dialysis: cramps, itch, sleep and restless legs

About 18 minutes  ·  4 checkpoints  ·  certificate

Why this module exists

People on dialysis carry a symptom burden that compares with advanced cancer, and the symptoms they rate as worst are rarely the ones we ask about. Cramps, itch, poor sleep and restless legs are all common, all at least partly treatable, and all routinely missed because nobody enquires.

Three things are worth holding onto before we go through them one at a time.

The first is that these four cluster. Itch keeps people awake. Restless legs keep people awake. Poor sleep then makes every other symptom feel worse and makes low mood more likely. Treating one of them often improves another, which means the order you work in matters.

The second is that patients underreport all four. Most assume it simply comes with the territory, and many mentioned it once, nothing happened, and they stopped mentioning it. Asking directly on a round is a large part of the intervention.

The third is uncomfortable. The drugs with the best evidence for two of these four symptoms are gabapentin and pregabalin, which Module 4 flags as the commonest reversible cause of falls on this ward. Both of those statements are true at the same time, and the whole difference lies in the dose and the timing.

Cramps

A painful, sustained, involuntary contraction, usually in the calves or feet, most often in the second half of a run or in the hours afterwards. Some patients also cramp at night. It is one of the commonest reasons a session gets stopped early, which then leaves the patient underdialyzed and still overloaded.

The mechanism is not fully worked out, but the reliable associations are with how much fluid is being removed and how fast. Cramping late in every session usually means one of two things. Either you are removing too much too quickly, or you are pulling the patient below their true dry weight. Hyponatremia, low magnesium, low calcium, peripheral vascular disease and carnitine deficiency all contribute in some patients.

Work through it in this order

  1. Treat it as a fluid prescription problem first. The answer is nearly always to lower the rate at which fluid is being taken off, either by removing less, which means revisiting a target weight that is probably too low, or by taking longer over it with a longer or an extra session. Cutting sodium reduces the gain between runs so there is less to remove in the first place. If the patient still makes urine, regular furosemide on the non dialysis days reduces how much accumulates, so less has to come off at the session. All of this belongs to nephrology, but flagging the pattern is yours.
  2. Check the reversible chemistry. Magnesium, calcium and sodium. Replace magnesium intravenously rather than orally, since oral magnesium causes diarrhea in most of these patients and that makes the magnesium worse. Nephrology can arrange it during a run.
  3. Know what happens in the moment. The unit will stop ultrafiltration and give a small saline bolus. That is their call rather than yours, but it explains why a patient sometimes comes back from a session having had less fluid removed than planned.
  4. Understand the trade-offs nephrology weighs. Raising the dialysate sodium does reduce cramps, but it increases thirst, weight gain between runs and blood pressure, so it buys one problem with another. Cooling the dialysate helps hypotension more than it helps cramps.
  5. Low risk adjuncts. Stretching before and during sessions is free and reasonable.
Do not prescribe quinine

Quinine does reduce cramps, which is why it was used for decades and why it still turns up on medication lists. It also causes thrombocytopenia, hemolytic uremic syndrome, arrhythmia and death, and regulators have specifically warned against using it for this indication.

Do not simply stop it overnight. It works well enough that patients who have taken it for years will struggle without it, and an abrupt discontinuation with no conversation produces a very unhappy patient and a difficult shift for their nurse. Frame it as a plan rather than a decision, along the lines of it no longer being recommended because of the side effects, so the aim is to reduce it with a view to stopping.

Tonic water is worth mentioning as a substitute, since it contains far less quinine, provided the fluid volume is not an issue for them.

Make sure it really is a cramp

A cramp is painful, the muscle is visibly or palpably knotted, and it eases with stretching the muscle out. Restless legs are not painful in the same way, involve an urge to move rather than a contraction, and ease with walking about. Neuropathy burns and is fairly constant. Claudication comes on with walking and stops with rest, which is the opposite pattern. The table in the last section of this module lays these out side by side.

Checkpoint 1 of 4
A patient cramps badly in the last hour of every run and has needed the session stopped early twice this month. What is the first move?

It is a fluid prescription problem. Cramping consistently at the end of a run points at either removing too much too fast or pulling the patient below their real dry weight, and both are fixed by changing the prescription rather than adding a drug. Longer or extra sessions, a higher target weight, and less sodium so there is less to take off.

Quinine works but causes thrombocytopenia, hemolytic uremic syndrome and arrhythmia, and is specifically warned against for cramps. Gabapentin at 900 mg a day in someone with no kidney function is a fall waiting to happen. Magnesium is worth checking and replacing if genuinely low, but not giving blindly.

Note the loop this creates if you leave it. Sessions cut short means underdialyzed and still overloaded, which means a bigger volume to remove next time, which means more cramping.

Itch

Around four in ten dialysis patients itch and roughly one in five itch severely. It is independently associated with poor sleep, depression and higher mortality, and it is one of the most under-asked symptoms in medicine.

The cause is multifactorial and still not fully settled. Dry skin, systemic inflammation, an imbalance between mu and kappa opioid receptor signalling in the skin and central nervous system, retained uremic solutes, and mineral bone disease all appear to contribute. That mixed picture is why no single drug fixes it and why the approach is a ladder.

Look at the skin before you treat

Itch from kidney disease has no primary rash. What you see is dry skin and the marks of scratching. If there is an actual rash, that is a different diagnosis, most often a drug reaction, scabies or eczema. Also worth excluding are cholestasis, iron deficiency, thyroid disease and, in the right context, lymphoma. Ask about opioids too, because they cause itch directly and are widely prescribed here.

  1. Skin care. Emollients applied generously and regularly, short lukewarm showers rather than long hot ones, and a mild soap. This has the best ratio of benefit to risk of anything on the list and it is consistently under-prescribed, partly because it feels too simple to bother writing down.
  2. Topical menthol and camphor. Worth trying early. It is a counter irritant, so it gives the brain a different sensation to attend to rather than fixing anything underlying, but it helps some patients a great deal and carries essentially no risk.
  3. Optimize what can be optimized. Dialysis adequacy, and the phosphate, calcium and parathyroid hormone from Module 4. The evidence that fixing these fixes itch is modest, but they need attention anyway.
  4. Gabapentin or pregabalin. These carry the strongest randomized evidence for moderate to severe itch. They must be renally dosed at doses well below what you would use in anyone else, so ask the nephrology pharmacist. Give it at bedtime, including on dialysis days. Dosing it in the daytime, including straight after a run, is what leads to people falling on the unit. Start low, titrate slowly, and check for unsteadiness at every encounter.
  5. Narrowband ultraviolet B phototherapy. Has evidence behind it, but the practical barriers of getting a dialysis patient to a third appointment each week are considerable.
Antihistamines do not work for this

Histamine is not the main driver of itch in kidney failure, and the evidence for antihistamines in this setting is poor. They are nonetheless prescribed constantly, because itch feels like it should be a histamine problem.

The sedating ones do real harm. Diphenhydramine and hydroxyzine cause falls, delirium and daytime sedation in a population already at high risk of all three, and they make restless legs worse, which you will meet in the last section. If you find one on a medication list, stopping it is usually more valuable than anything you could add.

Checkpoint 2 of 4
A 66 year old on hemodialysis has itched for months. There is no rash, just dry skin and scratch marks. She takes diphenhydramine 25 mg at night, which she says does not really help. What is the most appropriate step?

Stop the antihistamine and start at the bottom of the ladder. Histamine is not the main driver here, which is exactly why it is not helping her, and the sedating antihistamines carry a genuine risk of falls and delirium as well as making restless legs worse.

Emollients used properly are the highest value first step and are almost always missing. If that is not enough, gabapentin has the best randomized evidence, given at something like 100 mg after each session rather than a daily dose, with a clear warning about drowsiness.

The absence of a rash is the useful finding. Itch from kidney disease has no primary rash, so dry skin and excoriations fit, and a rash would have sent you looking for a drug reaction or scabies instead.

Sleep

Somewhere between half and three quarters of dialysis patients report sleeping badly. It is very rarely primary insomnia, and it is nearly always worth working through, because most of what causes it can be treated.

Find the cause before reaching for a drug

  • Sleep apnea, which is strikingly common here. Far more prevalent than in the general population, and both obstructive and central forms occur. Part of the reason is that fluid shifts out of the legs and into the neck when a patient lies down, narrowing the upper airway, which is also why volume control and nocturnal dialysis both improve it. Ask about snoring, witnessed pauses, morning headache and unrefreshing sleep, and note that blood pressure that will not settle despite being at target weight is a clue.
  • The other three symptoms in this module. Itch and restless legs are two of the commonest reasons a dialysis patient cannot get to sleep. Treating them is treating the insomnia, and it is worth asking about both before concluding that sleep is the problem.
  • The schedule itself. Early morning shifts, long transport times, and sleeping through a four hour run all disrupt the sleep and wake cycle. Napping on the machine is not neutral.
  • Everything else. Depression, pain, caffeine, alcohol, and uremic symptoms from inadequate dialysis.

Then treat

  • Cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia is first line and has randomized evidence specifically in dialysis populations. It is the only intervention on this page with a good effect size and no toxicity. Ask what is available locally, because access rather than evidence is the limiting factor.
  • Sleep hygiene, including actively discouraging sleep during dialysis, keeping the bedroom for sleep, and cutting caffeine after the morning.
  • Exercise programs, including intradialytic exercise, improve sleep quality in several small trials.
  • Melatonin 3 mg has modest randomized evidence in hemodialysis for getting to sleep. Low risk and reasonable to try once the causes above have been addressed.
  • Where something more is needed, we generally reach for trazodone or mirtazapine rather than a hypnotic, since the concerns about falls and dependence are much smaller. You may also see one of the newer orexin antagonists on an admission medication list. They are not on our formulary, so a patient already established on one will need to bring in their own supply.
Avoid benzodiazepines and the Z drugs

Falls, fractures, delirium and daytime sedation, in a group already at high risk of every one of them. Zopiclone and its metabolites accumulate in kidney failure, so the morning after is worse than you expect.

If a sedative hypnotic is already on the list, deprescribing it carefully is usually a higher value intervention than adjusting the dose. That is a genuinely useful thing to start during an admission, when there is time and supervision.

Checkpoint 3 of 4
A patient complains of months of poor sleep and daytime tiredness. He snores heavily, wakes with headaches, and his blood pressure remains high despite being reliably at his target weight. What is the best next step?

Screen for apnea. Snoring, morning headache, unrefreshing sleep and hypertension that will not settle in someone who is genuinely at target weight is a classic picture, and sleep apnea is far more common in dialysis patients than in the general population.

The gabapentinoid tension, resolved

Gabapentin and pregabalin are first line for two of the four symptoms in this module and are simultaneously the commonest reversible cause of falls on this ward. Both are true, and they are not in conflict.

Doses well below what you would use in anyone else, given at bedtime rather than in the daytime, started low, titrated slowly, and checked for drowsiness and unsteadiness at every encounter. What is actually yours in all four

Ask about them, because nobody else will. Look at the skin. Go through the medication list for the antihistamines, the sedative hypnotics and the antidopaminergics that are making things worse. Start emollients. Check iron studies with the restless legs threshold in mind. Screen for sleep apnea. Then escalate the prescribing decisions, having already removed the things that were working against you.

Peritoneal dialysis

About 20 minutes  ·  4 checkpoints  ·  certificate

What peritoneal dialysis actually is

Most hospitalists have never watched a peritoneal dialysis exchange. Since you will never prescribe or run one yourself, the aim here is just to understand what is happening in the room and to know which observations need escalating.

Instead of pumping blood out through a machine, peritoneal dialysis uses the lining of the patient's own abdominal cavity as the filter. That lining, the peritoneum, has a rich blood supply running immediately behind it, so anything sitting in the abdominal cavity is separated from the bloodstream by only a thin membrane. Put the right fluid in and the same exchange that happens in a dialyser happens inside the patient.

Getting fluid in and out requires a soft silicone tube called a Tenckhoff catheter. It is tunnelled through the abdominal wall so that the tip sits down in the pelvis, and it has one or two cuffs along its length which tissue grows into and which anchor it and act as a barrier to infection tracking inwards.

One exchange, three phases

Dialysis fluid runs into the abdomen through the catheter, sits there for a few hours, then drains back out under gravity. That whole cycle is called an exchange.

Icodextrin is an alternative to glucose, made from starch, and it is used for whichever dwell lasts longest. It pulls fluid more slowly and more steadily, which suits a dwell running eight to twelve hours where glucose would have been absorbed and stopped working long before the end. It has one important implication for the ward, which comes up later in this module.

The two ways patients do this

Patients organize the exchanges in one of two ways, and knowing which your patient uses tells you when problems will show themselves.

Why anyone chooses this

It is a home therapy, so there are no needles, no vascular access and no three trips a week to a unit. Because clearance happens continuously rather than in bursts, it is gentler on the circulation and produces far fewer of the blood pressure swings from Module 2. It also preserves residual renal function better than hemodialysis does, which is the main reason nephrotoxins matter even more in these patients. Whatever urine they still make is doing real work.

Checkpoint 1 of 4
Your patient tells you she does the machine at night and carries one bag through the day. What is she describing?

CCPD, also called APD. A bedside cycler runs four or five exchanges while she sleeps, and she then leaves a single long dwell in her abdomen through the day, which is very often icodextrin because it holds up better over eight to twelve hours.

CAPD is the other version, where four or five exchanges are done manually by hand across the day with no machine at all. Knowing which one your patient uses tells you when the exchanges are happening, and therefore when a drainage problem will first show itself.

Your job when a PD patient is admitted

Four ward rules
  1. PD must not simply stop. Nephrology and the PD nurse arrange for it to continue in hospital. What is yours is notifying them on the day of admission and noticing if exchanges are not actually happening. If PD has to be paused for a procedure, nephrology arranges the alternative, and there should never be a silent gap.
  2. Only trained staff touch the catheter. Contamination during connection is a leading cause of peritonitis, and the catheter is never used for anything other than PD.
  3. Every PD patient needs a bowel regimen and a documented bowel movement. This one is genuinely yours to order.
  4. Make sure every exchange is recorded. The daily net fluid removed is this patient's fluid balance and belongs on the chart alongside their intake.

One thing that is easier than in hemodialysis. You do not need to think about the timing of medication doses relative to the exchanges. Everything the last five modules said about giving drugs after the run applies to hemodialysis and not here. The exception is intraperitoneal antibiotics, which go into the bag and are timed by the PD team.

Constipation really is a dialysis problem

This surprises people, so it is worth being explicit about why. A loaded colon sitting in the pelvis physically obstructs the holes at the end of the catheter, so fluid will not run in properly and will not drain out properly. What that looks like on the ward is drainage failure that appears mechanical. Separately, constipation raises the risk of peritonitis, because bacteria translocate across a distended bowel wall into the peritoneal cavity.

PD patients are unusually prone to constipation in the first place. Fluid restriction, phosphate binders, iron, calcium, low fibre renal diets and opioids all push in the same direction.

How to manage it
  • Prescribe proactively on admission rather than reactively on day four. Ask on every round when they last opened their bowels and write the answer down.
  • Polyethylene glycol is the mainstay, with senna alongside it, and lactulose as an alternative. We do not carry docusate and there is barely any evidence behind it, so if a patient comes in on it that is a easy opportunity to reduce their pill burden.
  • Do not stop at oral agents. Many PD patients arrive severely constipated, and glycerin or bisacodyl suppositories and tap water enemas are often what is actually needed.
  • If they are constipated and also magnesium deficient, which is common here, oral magnesium replacement solves both problems at once and works well.
  • Never magnesium citrate, never milk of magnesia, and never a sodium phosphate enema. The phosphate load is dangerous and the magnesium accumulates.
  • Be careful with advice to eat more fibre, since many high fibre foods are also high in potassium and phosphate. Involve the renal dietitian rather than suggesting bran.
  • When drainage fails, think about stool and catheter position before anything exotic. An abdominal film shows you both the faecal loading and where the tip has ended up.

Peritonitis

Cloudy effluent means peritonitis until proven otherwise. Abdominal pain is usual and fever is often absent, and patients very often notice it before we do, so take the report seriously.

Send the effluent for a cell count with differential, a Gram stain and culture, and inoculate blood culture bottles at the bedside as well as sending the bag, since the yield from bottles is considerably higher.

The diagnosis needs two of these three
  1. Clinical features that fit, meaning abdominal pain, cloudy effluent, or both
  2. Effluent white cell count above 100 per microlitre with more than 50 percent neutrophils, after a dwell of at least two hours
  3. A positive effluent culture

On a cycler the dwells are short, so the absolute count can read low and the neutrophil share is the more reliable cue. If the abdomen is dry, instil a litre, let it dwell an hour or two, then sample.

Treatment is where hospitalists most often go wrong. Antibiotics go into the peritoneal cavity rather than intravenously, because putting the drug directly where the infection is achieves far higher local concentrations than anything you can achieve through a vein. Empiric cover is gram positive plus gram negative, and the regimen, the doses and the dwell times come from nephrology using the local protocol. The PD nurse mixes and gives it. What is yours is recognising it, sending the right specimens, and calling straight away.

The catheter has to come out for fungal peritonitis, for infection that will not clear or keeps relapsing, and where there is a source inside the abdomen. That is a nephrology decision.

The one everybody forgets

Any peritoneal dialysis patient on antibiotics needs nystatin as fungal prophylaxis, and it continues until one week after the antibiotics finish. That applies to antibiotics for any indication, not just peritonitis.

The usual dose is nystatin 500,000 units four times daily, swish and spit.

Our PD peritonitis order set does not include it, so it has to be ordered separately every single time. It is worth checking this yourself rather than assuming the order set covered it.

The order set itself is completed by the nephrologist or the nephrology fellow, not by you, so you are not expected to fill it out while waiting for them to review.

This one is a surgical abdomen

Several organisms growing at once, or enteric organisms, or anaerobes, particularly alongside a high effluent amylase or a patient who is not improving, point to a surgical source rather than ordinary peritonitis. Think perforation, diverticulitis, appendicitis or cholecystitis. Image the abdomen and get surgery involved. Treating this as routine PD peritonitis is a fatal error.

Exit site and tunnel infections happen with PD catheters too, and the anatomy works the same way it does for hemodialysis catheters in Module 3. Again, the PD team manages them.

What else to anticipate

  • Glucose absorbed from the dialysate causes hyperglycemia, weight gain and raised triglycerides. Diabetic PD patients usually need more insulin than you would expect, and their requirement shifts whenever the prescription changes.
  • Protein is lost into the effluent, roughly 5 to 15 g a day and considerably more during peritonitis. A low albumin is therefore expected and is a nutrition issue rather than evidence about volume status. Refer to dietetics.
  • Hypokalemia is common here, which is the opposite of the hemodialysis reflex. Before reaching for a supplement, note that oral potassium is not covered by ODB and is expensive. Where it can be managed with high potassium foods, which the dietitians can help with, or by starting or increasing something that raises potassium anyway such as an ACE inhibitor or ARB, or a combination of the two, that is a better answer than a standing supplement.
  • Overload in a PD patient may mean lost residual renal function, a failing membrane, missed exchanges, a malfunctioning catheter or constipation. Nephrology adjusts the prescription with stronger glucose bags, icodextrin for the long dwell, or more frequent shorter exchanges. Add a loop diuretic if the patient still makes urine.
  • Hernias and leaks follow from having two litres of fluid raising the pressure inside the abdomen. Inguinal and umbilical hernias are common, and fluid can track into the chest and present as a one sided pleural effusion, usually on the right, with a high glucose concentration.
Icodextrin and glucose meters

Icodextrin is broken down to maltose, which falsely raises the reading on glucose meters that use the glucose dehydrogenase PQQ method. Patients have been given large doses of insulin for a hyperglycemia that was never there, with fatal consequences. Confirm on admission that the ward glucometer is compatible, and tell nursing and the diabetes team.

Checkpoint 3 of 4
A PD patient reports cloudy effluent and abdominal pain. The cell count comes back at 520 per microlitre with 80 percent neutrophils. What is the correct route of treatment?

Into the peritoneum. Putting the antibiotic directly into the cavity where the infection is achieves far higher local concentrations than any intravenous dose, and it is first line treatment for this.

You are not expected to write the order yourself, since the regimen, the doses and the dwell times are specific to the protocol and are set by nephrology, with the PD nurse mixing and administering. What is yours is recognising cloudy effluent for what it is, sending a cell count, Gram stain and culture including effluent into blood culture bottles, and calling nephrology immediately. Catheter removal is reserved for fungal infection, for disease that will not clear or keeps coming back, and for a surgical source.

The rest of the preparation

  • Empty bowels. Constipation makes insertion harder, raises the risk of perforating something, and predicts early drainage failure afterwards. Start the bowel regimen days beforehand rather than the night before.
  • Empty bladder immediately before insertion, since a distended bladder is what gets perforated during percutaneous placement.
  • Antibiotic prophylaxis, a single intravenous dose before the procedure per local protocol, usually cefazolin. Confirm with whoever is inserting.
  • Examine for hernias and document any previous abdominal surgery, since both change the route.
  • Exit site planning. The patient should be seen sitting and standing so the site avoids the beltline and any skin folds.
  • Consent covers bleeding, perforation of bowel or bladder, the catheter ending up in the wrong place, leaks, and the possibility of converting to an operation.
Afterwards, the break in period
  • Conventionally the catheter is left to heal for about two weeks before full volume dialysis starts, which lets the exit site and cuffs seal and reduces the risk of leaks.
  • Where dialysis cannot wait, low volume exchanges done lying flat can often start within 24 to 72 hours. Nephrology decides that, with the PD nurse involved from the outset.
  • Leave the initial dressing alone for roughly a week unless it is soiled, and keep the catheter immobilized so nothing pulls on the exit site.
  • Escalate straight away for fluid at the exit site or swelling under the skin, which means a leak, for bleeding, for drainage failure, and for anything suggesting bowel or bladder injury, meaning new abdominal pain, faeculent or bloody effluent, or a sudden urge to pass urine during a fill.
Checkpoint 4 of 4
A PD catheter insertion is booked with radiology in three days. Your patient takes apixaban for atrial fibrillation and ASA for secondary prevention. What do you arrange today?

Plan it today and confirm the interval with whoever is inserting. Apixaban clearance is markedly reduced in kidney failure, so the 24 to 48 hour holds you would use in someone with normal renal function do not apply. At least 48 to 72 hours is where the conversation starts, and pharmacy and radiology settle the final number.

ASA is often continued for percutaneous insertion, particularly for secondary prevention, but that is their call rather than an automatic stop. The general principle is that anticoagulation planning happens when the referral goes in, because a hold discovered on the morning of the procedure means a cancelled slot and a patient who still needs dialysis.
